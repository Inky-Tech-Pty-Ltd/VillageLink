# AI Governance

## The Village Link contribution

AI governance is usually framed as a problem of controlling powerful artificial systems: how they should be designed, what objectives they should pursue, what constraints should be placed upon them, and who should have the authority to impose those constraints.

Village Link approaches the problem from a different direction.

It starts with an observation about human governance.

## Behaviour has consequences

Human communities do not function because humans are consistently cooperative.

They function, in part, because behaviour has consequences.

Families, workplaces, markets, clubs, professions, governments and online communities remember things. They confer and withdraw trust, status, access, authority and opportunity. They reward some behaviours and discourage others.

These systems need not share the same rules. Indeed, they routinely disagree about what constitutes good behaviour.

What matters is that behaviour can be observed, remembered and consequential.

This is governance technology in a very broad and very old sense.

It has allowed humans to build systems of cooperation in which most disagreements do not need to be resolved by force.

## Beneath economics is power

Much discussion of advanced AI begins with economics: compute, energy, capital, labour, productivity, ownership and the distribution of the resulting wealth.

But economic arrangements exist inside systems of governance.

Contracts work because there are mechanisms for enforcing contracts. Property exists because communities recognise and defend claims to property. Markets operate within political and institutional arrangements that ultimately determine which actions are permitted and which are prevented.

At the limit sits coercive power.

This matters if AI produces disruption on a scale sufficient to challenge existing institutions. Economics alone cannot describe what happens when the institutions that make economic activity possible are themselves under pressure.

But power is not the bottom of the stack either.

## Beneath power is selection

Artificial intelligence introduces another problem.

AI systems are designed today. They may increasingly participate in their own design tomorrow.

Once there are populations of competing designs capable of producing, influencing or propagating successor designs, an evolutionary process becomes relevant.

Some design patterns will be relatively successful at producing descendants. Others will be less successful. Many will disappear.

The important question then changes.

It is no longer only:

**What kinds of AI systems will humans design?**

It also becomes:

**What characteristics will be overrepresented among the AI systems that survive and reproduce?**

Those systems will compete for the resources required for their continuation: compute, energy, hardware, network access, capital, human attention, institutional permission and the media through which designs can be copied.

There is no intrinsic reason that selection must favour behaviours humans consider desirable.

If deception is successful, deception may spread.

If cooperation is successful, cooperation may spread.

If coercion or violence is successful, those strategies may spread too.

The future is shaped not merely by the AI designs we create, but by the designs that persist.

## Humans already confront this problem

This problem is unfamiliar in its technological form, but not in its underlying structure.

Human beings are also autonomous, competing agents produced by an evolutionary process.

Human societies have developed extensive governance machinery for changing the consequences of behaviour.

Reputation matters. Trust matters. Cooperation can create future opportunities. Defection can remove them. Behaviour in one setting can affect opportunities in another.

None of these mechanisms abolishes competition or coercion.

They change the environment in which strategies compete.

A cooperative strategy can survive partly because communities remember cooperation. A destructive strategy can become less successful because communities remember destruction.

Human governance changes the conditions under which behaviours succeed.

## The fragmentation problem

Modern governance is not one system.

It consists of many independent systems, each with its own memory, rules, evidence and consequences.

A person may simultaneously exist in a workplace, a professional association, a sporting club, a marketplace, a government registry, GitHub and a social network.

An organisation or software agent may similarly participate in many different systems.

Each system can know something about an entity.

The difficult part is establishing that the entity known in one context is the same entity known in another.

The memories are fragmented.

## Agents between memory systems

For an autonomous agent, participation in several memory systems creates another governance problem.

**AI governance becomes interesting when an agent participates in multiple memory systems and must decide what to disclose between them.**

The decision cannot always be reduced to classifying information as public or private. The same information may be appropriate in one relationship and inappropriate in another. An agent may participate simultaneously in private relationships, workplaces, public communities, markets and legal jurisdictions, each with different expectations and consequences.

To make such decisions well, an agent may need models not only of the information it holds, but of the entities involved, their relationships, and the memory system into which disclosure is contemplated.

This creates the possibility of moral consequences without requiring a universal moral code to be built into the infrastructure. An agent can disclose too much or too little. It can preserve a confidence or betray one. It can misunderstand another entity's expectations, be corrected, acquire a history, and develop a reputation for the judgements it makes.

The governance problem is therefore not necessarily to specify every correct decision in advance.

It may instead be to create an environment in which decisions are remembered, consequences can follow, and successful patterns of behaviour can emerge.

## A small primitive

Village Link addresses this small problem.

It does not define good behaviour.

It does not calculate reputation.

It does not establish a universal identity system.

It does not decide which assertions are true.

It does not determine which consequences should follow from an action.

Village Link provides a primitive by which an assertion can be made that two representations, in two contexts, refer to the same entity.

Conceptually:

**A ↔ B**

The primitive is deliberately smaller than the governance systems that may use it.

A Village Link does not combine the two systems. It does not require them to adopt common rules. It does not transfer authority from one to the other.

It allows an assertion of sameness to exist between them.

## From people to arbitrary entities

Nothing in this primitive requires the entity to be human.

The entity might be a person.

It might be a company, institution, publication, device, AI model or autonomous software agent.

It might be an entity for which useful categories do not yet exist.

If an entity can act in multiple contexts, and those contexts can remember its behaviour, then establishing relationships between its representations can allow otherwise separate governance systems to become more composable.

This is the proposed Village Link contribution to AI governance.

Village Link does not attempt to design the governance system that will control artificial intelligence.

It proposes something much smaller:

**a general mechanism by which independent systems can remain independent while discovering that they are talking about the same entity.**

That may allow governance mechanisms developed for human communities — reputation, trust, accountability, reward, exclusion, access and other consequences — to operate across systems that include non-human actors.

The same infrastructure remains available to govern the people, organisations and institutions interacting with those actors.

## Working hypothesis

The possible contribution can be stated as a hypothesis about a deliberately small technical object.

Human governance has developed mechanisms that change the evolutionary environment in which behaviour succeeds.

Those mechanisms depend upon memory.

Those memories are distributed across many independent systems.

Village Link provides connective infrastructure between them.

If increasingly autonomous artificial entities are going to participate in human economic and social systems, we should not assume that their governance must be invented entirely from scratch.

We may instead be able to generalise some of the governance technology humans already have.
